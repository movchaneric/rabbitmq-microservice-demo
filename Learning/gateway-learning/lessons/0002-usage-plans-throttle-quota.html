Lesson 2 — Spend what Lesson 1 bought you

Usage plans: move the throttle from per-IP to per-identity, and add a quota.

Your gateway now knows who is calling — every request past apiKeyAuthMiddleware carries a req.caller. But your token-bucket rate limiter hasn't noticed. It still keys on req.ip, exactly as before Lesson 1. Today you connect the two: throttle by identity, and add the other half of an AWS usage plan — a quota, a volume cap your throttle alone can't express.

By the end: two callers on the free plan each get their own 5-request bucket (no more sharing a limit just because they're behind the same corporate NAT) — and a caller who's made 100 requests today gets a 429 with a quota message, even though their bucket has tokens to spare.

Throttle vs quota — two different limits, one usage plan

ConcernQuestion it answersAlready built?
ThrottleHow fast can this caller go right now? (rate + burst)Yes — your token bucket, Lessons before this track
QuotaHow much can this caller do today/this week/this month? (volume)No — today's new piece

AWS binds both to the same usage plan: a token-bucket throttle (rate/burst) and a quota (a slow counter with a longer window), both scoped to one API key. A caller can be well within their per-second rate and still get rejected because they've used up their daily allowance — that's why these are two separate mechanisms, not one.

Why the registry has to move to Redis now. Lesson 1 kept the registry as an in-memory Map on purpose — one gateway process, one map, no problem. A quota counter breaks that assumption: if you run more than one gateway instance (which any real deployment does, for redundancy), each instance's in-memory counter only sees its own slice of traffic. A caller could exhaust their real quota while each instance still thinks they're under it. The counter — and the registry that identifies who owns it — has to live somewhere every instance shares. That's what Redis is for here.

Check your grip before you build

In one sentence: what's the difference between a throttle and a quota?

A throttle limits how fast requests can arrive (tokens refilling per second, with a burst allowance). A quota limits total volume over a much longer window (e.g. 1,000/day) — a caller can be well under their throttle and still hit their quota.

Why can't a quota counter live in each gateway instance's own memory, the way Lesson 1's registry did?

With multiple instances behind a load balancer, each one only sees a fraction of a given caller's traffic. An in-memory counter per instance would undercount — the caller could exceed their real daily total while no single instance's counter shows it. The counter needs one shared source of truth, hence Redis.

Give one concrete way per-IP throttling misbehaves once you actually know caller identity.

Either direction is a real failure: many different callers behind one corporate NAT/proxy share a single IP and unfairly share one bucket (one noisy caller starves the rest) — or a single caller spread across many IPs (rotating proxies, multiple servers) gets a fresh bucket per IP and effectively escapes the limit entirely. Per-identity throttling fixes both.

Step 3 — the quota

New file: gateway/src/auth/usagePlanQuota.ts. A daily counter per caller, compared against a limit that depends on their plan. Same Redis pattern you already used in the /api/v1/_debug/redis-demo route (increment + expire) — just keyed on caller + day instead of a fixed demo key.
